Entry 03 the practical page

How to keep
one alive

Almost every orchid that dies indoors dies of kindness. Too much water, too much soil, too much attention. The trick is to remember what the plant actually is: a thing that lives on the side of a tree, in bright shade, in moving air, getting soaked and then dried.

Light — read the leaves

You don't need a light meter. The plant is one.

  • Mid grass-green, slightly glossy. Correct. Leave everything alone.
  • Deep, dark, luxurious green. Too little light. It looks wonderful and it will not flower.
  • Yellowish, or flushed red at the edges. Too much. A little red tinge on a Cattleya is fine; on a Phalaenopsis it means move it back.
  • Dry white or brown patch. Sunburn. It happened in an afternoon and it will not heal.

For most windowsill orchids, an east-facing window is the safe default: bright first thing, nothing harsh later. A south window works behind a net curtain. North-facing suits slippers and jewel orchids and disappoints everything else.

The old test still works: hold your hand a foot above the leaves at midday. A soft-edged shadow is about right. A sharp black shadow is too much; no shadow at all is too little.

If your windows are dim

A cheap LED grow light on a timer for 12 hours a day, sat 25–30 cm above the leaves, will grow a very happy Phalaenopsis through a northern winter. This is the single best money-to-result ratio in the hobby.

Water — the part everyone gets wrong

soak, then forget

Orchid roots are wrapped in velamen, a spongy sheath built to grab a rainstorm in seconds and then dry out in the wind. It is not built to sit wet. A root that stays saturated suffocates and rots, and a rotted root cannot be brought back.

The method

  1. Take the plant to the sink, out of its decorative outer pot.
  2. Run tepid water through the bark for 20–30 seconds, until it pours freely from the bottom.
  3. Let it drain properly — a minute or two, tipped slightly.
  4. Tip any water out of the crown, the little cup where the leaves meet. Water sitting there overnight causes crown rot, which is fatal and fast.
  5. Put it back. Do not water again until it needs it.

Knowing when it needs it

Three tests, in order of reliability:

  • Weight. Lift the pot. A watered orchid is noticeably heavy; a thirsty one feels like nothing. This is the best test there is.
  • Root colour. In a clear pot, wet roots are bright green, thirsty roots are silvery-white. Wait for silver.
  • A skewer. Push a wooden skewer deep into the bark, leave five minutes, pull it out. Damp and dark means wait.

In a normal centrally heated home this lands somewhere around once a week in summer and every ten to fourteen days in winter. Treat that as a starting guess, not a rule — and always water in the morning, so the leaves are dry by nightfall.

Enough about this

The ice cube thing

Three ice cubes a week is sold as foolproof. It isn't.

An orchid on a tree in the Philippines has never once encountered ice. Cold water against warm roots causes real damage over time, and three cubes melt down to roughly 45 ml — enough to wet the top of the bark and nothing else, so the roots at the bottom of the pot slowly desiccate while the surface stays permanently damp.

Air, humidity, food

the three quiet ones
Humidity

40 to 70 per cent

Most homes run drier than that, especially with heating on. Group plants together so they share their own moisture, or stand pots on a tray of pebbles with water below the pot base — never touching it. Bathrooms and kitchens are genuinely good rooms if they have light.

Airflow

A stirring, not a draught

Still humid air is how fungal spots and rots get started. A small fan on low across the room, or an occasional open window, does more for orchid health than almost anything else. Cold blasts straight onto the leaves, though, will drop buds overnight.

Misting

Mostly theatre

A spray bottle raises humidity for about four minutes and leaves water sitting in crowns and leaf joints, which is exactly where you don't want it. Skip it. If you want humidity, buy a tray or a humidifier.

Feeding

Weakly, weekly

Bark holds almost no nutrients, so orchids do need feeding — just very dilute. A balanced orchid feed at a quarter of the label strength, every second or third watering during active growth. Once a month, flush with plain water only, to wash out accumulated salts.

A basic epiphyte mix
  • 4 parts medium-grade bark
  • 1 part perlite, for air
  • 1 part horticultural charcoal, to keep it sweet
  • Optional handful of sphagnum if your home is very dry

Slippers and jewel orchids want finer bark with more moss. Vandas want no pot at all.

Potting, and when to repot

Bark is not soil — it is scaffolding. Its job is to hold the plant upright while letting air reach the roots, and it breaks down. After about two years it has composted into something dense and water-retentive, and that is what kills the plant, not neglect.

Repot every one to two years, or sooner if the mix has gone soft and dark, if it smells sour, or if the plant has climbed out of its pot entirely.

How

  1. Do it just after flowering finishes, when new roots are starting.
  2. Ease the plant out, wash the old bark off the roots, and cut away anything brown, hollow or slimy with a sterile blade.
  3. Choose a pot barely bigger than the root ball. Orchids flower better slightly cramped, and a large pot stays wet in the middle.
  4. Work fresh bark in around the roots and tap the pot to settle it. Don't press it down hard.
  5. Wait a week before watering, so cut surfaces can seal.

Sterilise the blade. A new razor blade each time, or a flame. Several orchid viruses spread on dirty secateurs and there is no cure — an infected plant is a plant you eventually bin.

Aerial roots

The silver-white roots waving in the air are not lost or unhappy. That is normal epiphyte behaviour and they are absorbing moisture from the air. Do not cut them off and do not force them into the pot.

Making it flower again

The commonest complaint about moth orchids is that they flower once, magnificently, and then just sit there being a green thing for two years. Two levers fix it.

1. A cold snap

Phalaenopsis set a new flower spike in response to cooler nights. From early autumn, give it four to six weeks where the night temperature falls to about 15–18 °C — five or six degrees below its daytime. A windowsill behind a closed curtain will often do it by itself. A porch or an unheated spare room does it reliably.

2. Cutting the old spike correctly

Once the last flower has dropped, look at the green stalk:

  • Still green and healthy? Cut it about 2 cm above a node (one of the little triangular bumps), roughly the second or third node up from the base. It will often branch from that node and flower again within a couple of months — smaller flowers, sooner.
  • Want the best display? Cut the whole spike off at the base instead. The plant puts its energy into leaves and roots, then throws a full new spike next season — later, but far better.
  • Gone yellow, brown or hollow? It is finished. Remove it at the base.

Sympodial orchids — Cattleya, Dendrobium, Oncidium, Cymbidium — work differently: they flower from newly matured growth, so the lever there is finishing each season's growth well, and then giving many of them a cool, dry rest before they will bud.

Do
  • Water in the morning, at the sink, until it runs through
  • Let it dry out before watering again
  • Empty the crown after every water
  • Keep it in a clear plastic pot inside the pretty one
  • Feed dilute, and flush monthly
  • Repot into fresh bark every year or two
Don't
  • Stand it in a saucer of water
  • Pot it in ordinary compost
  • Use ice cubes
  • Cut the aerial roots off
  • Repot the moment you get it home, if it's in flower
  • Move it about — buds drop when conditions change

Limp, leathery leaves Root loss from overwatering — the plant is thirsty because it can no longer drink Unpot. Cut every dead root away, repot into fresh bark, water sparingly while it rebuilds
Buds yellow and drop before opening Bud blast: a sudden change — draught, move, heat, dry air, ripening fruit nearby Put it somewhere stable and leave it there. Keep fruit bowls away; ethylene gas aborts buds
Soft black patch at the centre of the leaves Crown rot from water left sitting in the crown Act today. Cut out all rotted tissue with a sterile blade, dust with cinnamon, keep dry and airy
Sticky leaves, white fluff in leaf joints Mealybugs Dab each one with a cotton bud dipped in surgical spirit, then repeat weekly for a month

Healthy plant, no flowers for over a year Not enough light, or no seasonal temperature drop Move it brighter, then give it cool autumn nights for a month
Wrinkled pseudobulbs Genuine underwatering, or dead roots Check the roots first. If they are sound, water more often
Streaked or mosaic-patterned leaves, sunken marks Possible orchid virus Isolate it immediately, sterilise anything that touched it. There is no treatment

A dead orchid taught me more than five living ones. Take the pot apart and look at the roots — the whole story of what you did is written down there.
