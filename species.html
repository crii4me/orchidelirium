Entry 02 learning the faces

Who's who
in the family

Twenty-eight thousand species is not a number anyone can hold. But roughly a dozen genera cover almost everything you will meet in a shop, a show bench or a friend's kitchen, and they are all recognisable from the doorway.

First, how to read the label

Orchid names look intimidating and are actually a tidy little system. A tag reading Phalaenopsis Sogo Yukidian 'V3' breaks down like this:

  • Phalaenopsis — the genus. Always italic, always capitalised.
  • Sogo Yukidian — the grex: the name given to every seedling from that particular cross of parents. Roman type, capitalised, never italic.
  • 'V3' — the cultivar: one specific plant, singled out and then cloned. In single quotes.

If the second word is lower-case and italic — Phalaenopsis amabilis — it is a wild species, not a hybrid. Species are the purists' pleasure; hybrids are usually tougher, cheaper and flower for longer.

Shorthand you'll see

Phal. Phalaenopsis · Catt. Cattleya · Paph. Paphiopedilum · Onc. Oncidium · Den. Dendrobium · Cym. Cymbidium

A multiplication sign in front of a name (×Brassocattleya) means the genus itself is a hybrid between two others. The Victorians started this and nobody has been able to stop them.

Twelve to know by sight

field guide
Monopodial · warm · easy

Moth orchid

Phalaenopsis

The supermarket orchid, and genuinely one of the best houseplants there is. Flat wings, arching spray, and blooms that can hold for three or four months. Wants low light, steady warmth, and a night that is a few degrees cooler than the day to set a new spike.

Sympodial · bright · moderate

Corsage orchid

Cattleya

Huge frilled trumpet lips, often scented like sherbet at breakfast time. This is the flower on every 1950s prom photograph. Give it the brightest window you own and let it dry out hard between drinks.

Sympodial · low light · easy

Slipper orchids

Paphiopedilum & Phragmipedium

Two genera, one idea: a waxy pouch slung under a hood, with a sterile shield called a staminode where the column would be. Neither has pseudobulbs, so neither should ever dry out completely.

Paphiopedilum is the Asian one — broad striped dorsal sepal, short horizontal petals. Mottled leaves want warmth, plain green leaves want it cooler.

Phragmipedium is tropical American, and the giveaway is the petals: long twisting ribbons that in some species keep growing for months. Many like their feet permanently wet, standing in a shallow tray of water — almost unique in this family.

Sympodial · bright · moderate

Dancing ladies & Cambrias

the Oncidium alliance

The classic Oncidium throws dozens of small yellow-and-brown flowers on a branching spray, each a tiny figure in a full skirt. The hybrid Sharry Baby smells unmistakably of chocolate.

The alliance around it is a tangle of intergeneric hybrids — sold under trade names like Cambria — with far bigger flowers, heavily blotched in magenta and cream. Modern taxonomy has folded Odontoglossum into Oncidium, so a great many of them are now, officially, Oncidiums too.

Monopodial vine · hot · hard

Vanilla

Vanilla planifolia

A climbing orchid that will run twelve metres up a tree, and the only orchid grown as a food crop. Each pale green flower opens for a single morning. Growable indoors; fruiting indoors, essentially never.

Sympodial · varies wildly · varies

Dendrobium

Dendrobium

Around 1,800 species, which makes "how do I care for my Dendrobium?" an unanswerable question. The two you will meet: soft-cane nobile types, which demand a cold dry winter to flower, and the warm-growing phalaenopsis types sold as cut stems.

Sympodial · bright · needs space

Cymbidium

Cymbidium

Strappy grass-like leaves and waxy flowers that last for weeks — the classic corsage and hotel-lobby orchid. Notoriously will not bloom unless it spends late summer outdoors, feeling a real gap between day and night temperatures.

Monopodial · blazing light · hard

Vanda

Vanda

Grown hanging in an empty basket with its roots in mid-air, because that is exactly how it lives. Needs drenching daily and light most homes cannot supply, and repays it with flat flowers the size of a hand — including a genuinely blue one, V. coerulea.

Sympodial · cool · fussy

Pansy orchid

Miltoniopsis

Flat, open faces with a blotched mask, and a strong rose scent. From the cool cloud forests of Colombia and Ecuador, so a hot flat kills them. If your house is chilly, this is your chance to be smug.

Sympodial · humid · obsessive

Bulbophyllum

Bulbophyllum

The largest genus in the family, with well over 2,000 species and a strong commitment to being strange: hinged lips that rock in the breeze, whiskers, dangling tails, and a great many flowers that smell of rotting meat because their pollinator is a fly.

Sympodial · cold · fussy

Masdevallia

Masdevallia

Andean miniatures whose sepals fuse into a triangle and then trail off into three long tails. They want cool, wet, constantly moving air — conditions that suit a mountain and almost no living room.

Terrestrial · shade · easy

Jewel orchid

Ludisia discolor

The one you grow for its leaves: dark velvet-maroon, veined in pink like something stitched. Flowers are small and white and frankly beside the point. Happy in a shady corner and a normal pot of compost, which makes it the family rebel.

A word about alliances

Twelve tidy genera is a convenient lie. In practice orchid growers talk about alliances — clusters of closely related genera that cross with each other so freely that the boundaries between them stopped meaning very much a century ago.

The Cattleya alliance is the great example. Cattleya interbreeds with Laelia, Brassavola, Guarianthe, Encyclia, Rhyncholaelia and more besides, which is why plant labels sprout those alarming compound names: ×Brassocattleya, ×Rhyncholaeliocattleya, ×Laeliocattleya. Each one records which genera went into the pot.

The flower here is one of those relatives rather than a Cattleya proper — narrow spotted tepals instead of broad ruffled ones, but the same wide blotched lip and the same trumpet throat. Put it beside the magenta Cattleya further up this page and the family resemblance is unmistakable, which is precisely the point: they are close enough to breed, so they are close enough to look alike.

The same thing happens around Oncidium, where an entire alliance of intergeneric hybrids is sold under trade names like Cambria, and around Phalaenopsis, which crosses happily with Doritis and Vanda. If a name on a label looks like two words fused with a collision, an alliance is what you are looking at.

The show-offs

records, oddities, liars

The ghost

Dendrophylax lindenii has no leaves at all. It is a tangle of flat green roots clamped to a swamp tree, photosynthesising through the roots themselves, and once a year it produces a white flower that appears to hover in the dark. Florida and Cuba only, and the reason at least one person a year gets arrested in the Fakahatchee Strand.

The one that flowers underground

Rhizanthella gardneri, of Western Australia, spends its entire life below the surface. It has no chlorophyll, lives off a fungus that is itself attached to a broom bush, and opens its flower head in the soil, where it is thought to be pollinated by termites and tiny flies. It was first found in 1928 by a farmer ploughing a field.

The impersonator

The bee orchid, Ophrys apifera, grows a lip that looks and smells like a female solitary bee. In Britain the trick has become redundant — the right bee doesn't live here, so our bee orchids quietly pollinate themselves instead, still wearing the costume.

The biggest and the smallest

The tiger orchid, Grammatophyllum speciosum, forms clumps that have been recorded at well over a tonne, with flower spikes taller than a person. At the other end, Platystele jungermannioides opens flowers around two millimetres across — you need a hand lens to confirm it has bloomed at all.

Darwin's moth

Angraecum sesquipedale, the Madagascan star orchid, hides its nectar at the bottom of a spur nearly 30 cm long. In 1862 Darwin argued that a moth must exist with a tongue long enough to reach it. The moth — now called Xanthopan praedicta, "the predicted one" — was formally described in 1903.

Take this to a plant shop

If you have never kept one

  • Buy a Phalaenopsis. Not a compromise — it genuinely is the right answer.
  • Buy it in bud, not in full flower, and you get months more from it.
  • Check the roots through the clear pot: firm and silvery-green is good, brown mush is not.
  • Skip anything sold in moss packed tight into a decorative pot with no drainage.
